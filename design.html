How this site is designed

This is a photography site first. Each section below is one decision behind it: the problem, what was built, and what changed when it was measured. Every demo runs the site's own code. Press D to see the strip's layout drawn over it.

  • A strip instead of a grid
  • Photos arrive as their own colors
  • A dial for the whole strip
  • Shuffled, but never the same film twice in a row
  • Springs, not timers
  • Contact

A strip instead of a grid

Problem
A grid gives every photo the same box, whatever its shape.
Decision
One strip that moves sideways. Each photo takes its size, height and spacing from three short patterns of different lengths, so neighbors rarely line up the same way twice. Tall photos get more of the height, wide ones less.
Trade-off
A phone has no room for that, so there every photo starts at the same height with the same gap, and the wide ones fill the screen's width.

Under each photo: its share of the height, and how far down it starts (0 is the top, 1 the bottom). In the gaps: the space before it, in page margins. Dashed lines: the top and bottom a photo can reach.

Photos arrive as their own colors

Problem
The first visit to Photographs fetched only the photos near the screen. Coming back fetched all the rest: about 166 MB. Photos off screen were marked to load later, but each was given its address before that instruction, so a browser that remembered them started all of them at once.
Decision
Set the address last. Make two smaller copies of every photo, and let each screen pick the smallest that looks sharp. Until a photo arrives, the average colors of its top and bottom halves hold its place.
Trade-off
The first smaller copies kept 75% of the film grain. Raising their quality kept 85%, for about 40% more data, and that's the one the site uses. I shoot film, and the grain is part of the picture. Smoothing it away to save space would change the photo.

A dial for the whole strip

Problem
At 1440 by 900 the strip was about 192 screens long, and all that showed where you were was a pale scrollbar handle about 17 pixels wide.
Decision
A dial like the one on a camera: one tick for each photo, in that photo's own colors, turning under a fixed needle. Drag, swipe, flick or tap it, or use the arrow keys. The frame number and film stock say where you are.
Trade-off
The order is shuffled on every visit, so the dial can't point to a particular roll. It says how far along you are, and which film you're looking at.
Result
Arrow keys reach every photo, and a few flicks cross the whole strip. Photos that only fly past show their colors and download nothing. On a 120 Hz screen the dial drew a frame every 8.3 ms, and none took longer than 17 ms.

Shuffled, but never the same film twice in a row

Problem
The strip mixes film stocks so that every visit shows a variety. The rolls are uneven, though, so a plain shuffle puts two of the same film side by side about 48 times.
Decision
Shuffle each roll, then deal from a random roll that isn't the one just dealt, favoring the rolls with the most left.
Trade-off
If one roll outnumbers all the others put together, it has to be dealt next, or its photos would pile up at the end.

Springs, not timers

Problem
A timed animation follows a fixed path. Change where it's going halfway and it starts again from rest, so pressing back mid-move jerks.
Decision
Scrolling and the dial ride springs, which know only where they are, how fast they're going and where they're headed. Let go of the dial mid-flick and it carries on at the speed of your hand.
Trade-off
The ends of a page stay hard stops, because faking a bounce there would fight the browser's own scrolling. If your system asks for less motion, everything jumps straight to its end.
Result
Ordinary scrolling follows your hand as closely as it did before. A notch of the wheel settles about 0.2 seconds sooner, and an arrow key's step about a third of a second sooner.
